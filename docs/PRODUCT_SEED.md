# Experimental Earth product seed

## Investigate a real place and improve it

Give people a practical way to explore how their surroundings behave and how a change might help. Bring a real place into an interactive model, try alternatives, identify consequential uncertainty, make a useful measurement and revisit the decision. The model should become more useful through those encounters, not merely more detailed on screen.

A schoolyard is uncomfortable in summer and retains standing water after rain. Students, caretakers and neighbors explore the actual site together. They move a proposed shade structure, change a surface, investigate planting or consider a different flow path. The experience reveals possible effects on heat, water, vegetation and usable space, then identifies a missing soil measurement that could reverse the comparison. Someone can question the assumptions, contribute observations and compare a different objective.

These are concrete openings, not an exhaustive feature list or a fixed destination. Extend the product, redesign its experience and investigate capabilities this seed does not anticipate.

## Build a useful account of the place

Combine authorized photographs, scans, maps, terrain data, weather observations, sensor records and manual measurements. A person should be able to recognize the place, inspect features that matter to their question and see where information is missing. Keep source, date, units, spatial resolution and measurement uncertainty connected to the model.

Recover or import geometry such as slopes, boundaries, structures, surfaces and vegetation. Separate measured properties from estimates and illustrative assumptions. An attractive texture does not establish soil permeability; an ordinary photograph does not measure surface temperature. Help people improve an incomplete account rather than requiring a comprehensive professional survey before anything is useful.

## Make alternatives explorable

Let people change an arrangement or propose an intervention and compare consequences across space and time. They might inspect shade at different times, investigate where water collects, compare surface materials, examine a planting arrangement or explore how several small changes interact. Preserve the original place and make alternatives easy to revise, combine or discard.

The interface should connect visible effects to the mechanisms and assumptions that produce them. Someone can ask why a location changes, what remains uncertain, or which other part of the site could be affected. Show useful ranges and competing explanations when a single confident animation would conceal disagreement. Keep the experience understandable and engaging rather than making every interaction a warning or a technical report.

## Turn uncertainty into a useful experiment

Suggest observations that would meaningfully distinguish alternatives: another elevation measurement, a soil test, a sensor at a particular location, a shaded comparison or a record of the site's response to weather. Explain what each observation could resolve and whether a cheaper or less intrusive alternative might suffice. People choose what to do within their authority; suggestions are not permission to modify land or deploy equipment.

Compare predicted and observed responses. A changed prediction after a measurement can be valuable progress, even when the original recommendation was wrong. Retain the earlier model and reasoning so people can understand the correction. Repeated measurements, reversible trials and appropriately controlled comparisons can contribute different kinds of evidence without becoming a mandatory sequence for every project.

## Couple processes without pretending everything is known

The hard work includes relating terrain, water movement, infiltration, storage, heat exchange, vegetation and changing weather at useful scales. Explore conservation constraints, coupled physical models, learned components and other approaches that can be tested. Make boundary conditions and omitted processes visible when they matter to a result.

Parameter identification is part of the problem. Different combinations of soil properties, surface conditions or unobserved flows can explain the same observation. Research how to retain plausible alternatives, propagate uncertainty and choose measurements that distinguish them. Faster approximations should be evaluated against appropriate reference calculations and actual observations; smooth animation is not a measure of physical accuracy.

Develop ways to compare changed conditions without attributing every difference to an intervention. Rainfall, season, maintenance and sensor error can alter the result. Prediction on observations not used for fitting, comparison with simpler methods and informative failures should help reveal what the model actually contributes.

## Support different people and decisions

Residents may want a more comfortable courtyard; a school may want an outdoor learning environment; a grower may investigate water retention; a community may compare proposals for a shared space. These uses can reveal different capabilities rather than requiring one generic sustainability dashboard.

Let people state and revise objectives such as comfort, habitat, maintenance, water use, access, cost or appearance. Improvements can conflict. Keep those trade-offs inspectable instead of hiding them inside one opaque score. Share an annotated alternative, its assumptions and the measurements that would improve confidence. Protect private locations and recordings, and distinguish a shared site observation from personal information or a permission to intervene.

Environmental experiments can affect neighboring places and living systems. Keep proposed changes distinct from real interventions, support appropriate human and expert judgment, and do not present a simulation as an engineering approval or a guarantee of safety.

## Let measurements and methods improve one another

Each measured weather event or authorized intervention can refine local parameters and expose missing mechanisms. Better models can suggest better experiments. Useful findings from another place may offer hypotheses, but need qualification against local conditions rather than automatic transfer. A failure can teach where a method is inappropriate or which assumption should be reconsidered.

Reuse suitable geometry, simulation, uncertainty and active-learning capabilities with their existing owners. Extend shared components when the product reveals a reusable need, while verifying that they improve this actual experience. No renderer, scientific model family, data vendor or platform rewrite is prescribed. An unavailable component should not become an invented gate on independent useful work.

## Meaningful progress and more possibilities

Useful evidence could be an independently measured prediction, an intervention whose observed effect supports a proposed mechanism, an anticipated adverse trade-off that changes a decision, or a measurement that overturns a misleading model. Clearly labeled simulation can establish bounded computational results while real-world questions remain open.

The exploration could grow toward seasonal planning, habitat relationships, community experiments, maintenance, changing land use, larger connected areas, and more. Let discoveries change the product's identity as well as its implementation. The point is to expand people's ability to understand and improve places, not to declare a universal digital twin complete.

Keep current outcomes and ownership on the [HAMON shared priority board](https://github.com/Jacob-Met/hamon/issues/1354) and existing project issues. These possibilities describe work worth exploring, not capabilities already delivered.
